Pollination is the transfer of pollen grains from anther to stigma. Autogamy refers to the transfer of pollen within the same flower, geitonogamy is the transfer of pollen between different flowers on the same plant, and xenogamy involves the transfer of pollen between flowers on different plants of the same species. Sunflowers are among my favourite flowers. At this size, we can observe them clearly. Sunflowers are typically pollinated by bees. However, when no bees are present, they have a clever mechanism to ensure pollination occurs. In sunflower heads, many florets are present, and their disc florets are generally protandrous: pollen is presented before the stigma becomes receptive. The anthers form a tube, and as the style elongates through that tube, it pushes or brushes pollen outward. The stigma lobes open and become receptive later. This is referred to as secondary pollen presentation and promotes outcrossing. In plants such as Ixora, the anther and the stigma reach maturity at the same time. This simultaneous maturation of the anther and the stigma facilitates self-pollination, a process known as homogamy. Some plants, like wood sorrel during late summer, produce small and unnoticeable flowers that lack fragrance. These bisexual flowers remain closed. The occurrence of self-pollination and fertilization within an unopened flower is called cleistogamy. In such plants, the pollen grain is already distributed on the stigma of the same flower to enable self-pollination. Cross-pollination occurs in papaya, a unisexual, dioecious plant. Male and female flowers grow on separate plants and typically undergo cross-pollination. However, sunflowers have bisexual flowers. Here, the anthers mature first, followed by the stigma. This process is known as protandry. On the other hand, in custard apple, the stigma matures before the anther. This process is referred to as protogyny. Some flowers, such as pansies, have a structure that spatially separates the anthers and the stigma within a flower. This structural adaptation is called herkogamy. In primrose, heterostyly is observed, where the stigma is located at a different height compared to the anther. These are various adaptations in flowers that aid cross-pollination. What draws a pollinator to a flower? Sometimes it's the sweet scent and the promise of nutritious nectar. Other times, it's deception. A flower known as Ophrys sphegodes mimics the chemical and floral signals of a female bee. This flower attracts male bees seeking a mate. When these males attempt to mate with the flower, they end up pollinating it. Fascinating, isn't it? Keep exploring to learn more. Keep learning. We believe in you.
