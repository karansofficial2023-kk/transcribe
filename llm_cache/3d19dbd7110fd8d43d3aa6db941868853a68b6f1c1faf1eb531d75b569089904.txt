Pollination is the transfer of pollen grains from anther to stigma. Autogamy refers to the transfer of pollen within the same flower, geitonogamy involves the transfer of pollen between different flowers on the same plant, and xenogamy is the transfer of pollen between flowers of different plants of the same species. Cleistogamy occurs in closed flowers, ensuring autogamy; examples include Viola, Oxalis, and Commelina. Dichogamy separates male and female maturity in time, with protandry meaning anthers mature first and protogyny indicating the stigma becomes receptive before pollen is released. Herkogamy is the spatial separation of anthers and stigma within a flower, reducing self-pollination. Heterostyly involves different floral forms with anthers and stigmas at varying heights; primrose is an accepted example.

Insect pollination includes visible adaptations such as nectar, scent, color, landing surfaces, and sticky pollen. Wind pollination uses abundant light dry pollen and exposed anthers and stigmas; grasses or maize are suitable examples. Water pollination is uncommon in flowering plants, with Vallisneria using detached male flowers that reach female flowers at the water surface, and Zostera using thread-like pollen transported underwater. Bird pollination involves robust, often red or orange, nectar-rich flowers. Bat pollination commonly involves large, sturdy, pale nocturnal flowers with strong scent and abundant nectar. Other animal pollination may involve beetles, butterflies, moths, flies, or small mammals.

Self-pollination offers advantages such as reproductive assurance, little dependence on pollinators, and preservation of successful gene combinations. However, it also has disadvantages including reduced genetic variation and possible inbreeding depression over generations. Cross-pollination provides greater genetic variation and potential adaptability but requires reliance on pollinators, involves greater pollen and energy expenditure, and may result in uncertain pollen transfer.

Sunflowers are among my favourite flowers. At this size, we can observe them clearly. Sunflowers are typically pollinated by bees. However, when no bees are present, they have a clever mechanism to ensure pollination occurs. In sunflower heads, many florets exist, with disc florets generally being protandrous: pollen is presented before the stigma becomes receptive. The anthers form a tube, and as the style elongates through that tube, it pushes or brushes pollen outward. The stigma lobes open and become receptive later.

In plants such as Ixora, the anther and the stigma reach maturity at the same time. This simultaneous maturation of the anther and the stigma facilitates self-pollination, a process known as homogamy. Some plants, like wood sorrel during late summer, produce small and unnoticeable flowers that lack fragrance. These bisexual flowers remain closed. The occurrence of self-pollination and fertilization within an unopened flower is called clistogamy. In such plants, the pollen grain is already distributed on the stigma of the same flower to enable self-pollination.

Cross-pollination occurs in papaya, a unisexual, dioecious plant where male and female flowers grow on separate plants and typically undergo average cross-pollination. However, sunflowers have bisexual flowers. In these flowers, the anthers mature first, followed by the stigma. This process is known as protandry. On the other hand, in custard apple, the stigma matures before the anther. This process is referred to as protogyny.

Some flowers, such as pansies, have a hood that covers the stigma. This structural adaptation is called herkogamy. Primrose exhibits heterostyly, where the stigma is located at a different height compared to the anther. These are various adaptations in flowers that aid cross-pollination.

What draws a pollinator to a flower? Sometimes it's the sweet scent and the promise of nutritious nectar. Other times, it's deception. A flower known as Ophrys sphegodes mimics the chemical and floral signals of a female bee, attracting male bees seeking a mate.
